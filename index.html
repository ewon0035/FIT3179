Australian Income and Wealth Distribution


The chart shows median income differences based on gender and industry. On average, males tend to earn higher median incomes across most industries, although the gap varies significantly by sector.

The gap varies the most in the education/training, financial/insurance services, information media/telecommunications and professional, scientific and technical services, where males earn substantially more. These sectors traditionally have higher overall salaries, suggesting the gender pay gap may be more pronounced in high-earning industries.

Interestingly, there are only two industries where females earn more than males: mining and administrative/support services. The mining sector anomaly is particularly notable given it's typically male dominated, which may reflect differences in specific roles or seniority levels held by women in this industry.

Industries with the smallest gender pay gaps tend to be in healthcare and retail trade, suggesting more equitable compensation structures in these sectors.

This chart shows the main sources of income by age group in Australia. Younger adults rely heavily on wages and salaries, whereas older adults increasingly depend on superannuation, investments, or government pensions.

The distribution highlights the lifecycle of income sources: early career income is wage-dominated, mid-career sees a mix of wages and investments, and retirement age is dominated by passive income streams. The transition becomes particularly pronounced around age 65, which aligns with Australia's traditional retirement age.

Between 20-64, employment income is by far the most common source of income, with as high as 76.6% of Australians citing it as their main source of income between 25-34 years old.

Notably, government pensions play a significant role in supporting older Australians, suggesting that despite superannuation policies, many retirees still depend on government support. For those under 25, there may also be notable reliance on government benefits such as youth allowance or parental financial support alongside entry-level wages.

This visualisation presents a comparison of median household incomes between metropolitan and regional areas across different states/territories. The data reveals a consistent pattern across all juridictions: metropolitan areas have higher median incomes compared to regional areas.

The Northern Territory shows the most pronounced disparity, with metro areas earning substantially more than regional areas. New South Wales also displays a significant gap, reflecting Sydney's position as Australia's economic powerhouse.

Queensland and Western Australia show moderate differences, likely influenced by their resource-rich regional economies that provide relatively well-paid mining and agricultural jobs.

Interestingly, Tasmania, South Australia, and Victoria demonstrate smaller gaps between metro and regional incomes. Tasmania's relatively compressed income distribution may reflect its smaller scale and less dramatic urban-rural divide, while Victoria's pattern could indicate Melbourne's economic dominance balanced against prosperous regional centers. South Australia similarly shows a modest gap, suggesting Adelaide's metro economy doesn't vastly outpace regional areas in the same way larger capital cities do.
